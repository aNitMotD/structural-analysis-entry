# A Note on the Sequence of General Structural Analysis

## 0. Purpose

This document is a note intended to organize **the basic sequence of structural analysis that may generally be in common use**.

The purposes of this document are as follows.

- To organize the sequence in which structural analysis may usually proceed.
- To distinguish what ordinary users commonly call “structure” from what it is actually closer to.
- To organize the general elements that can be shared at the entry level of structural analysis.
- However, it does not deal with the **proprietary formalization method** of a specific observational system, **higher-order coordinate design**, **the fixing method of lower observational units**, or **transition/cross-tag structures**.

That is, this document deals with **the entry of general structural analysis**, and is not a document that replaces the proprietary formalization document of a specific methodology.

---

## 1. Premise

Structural analysis usually begins from the following questions.

- What is being observed?
- What is it composed of?
- How is each element connected?
- Where does the state change?
- What repeats and what is exceptional?

Questions at this level belong to the range of relatively general analytical capability.

Therefore, this document does not regard structural analysis as something that **everyone performs in the same way**, but as an **entry-level capability that can be shared** across public materials, general analytical education, practical analysis, systems thinking, and qualitative structuring.

---

## 2. The Sequence of General Structural Analysis

General structural analysis may broadly be organized in the following sequence.

### 2.1 Defining the object

First determine what is to be analyzed.

Examples:

- event
- document
- organizational phenomenon
- system
- interaction
- failure case

The core of this stage is to **fix the target of analysis as one unit**, without yet excessively adding meaning to it.

### 2.2 Separating the components

Divide the main elements that make up the target.

Examples:

- actor
- object
- input
- output
- rule
- resource
- condition
- limiting factor

At this stage, rather than immediately drawing a conclusion about the whole, first unfold **what is contained in it**.

### 2.3 Identifying the relations

Examine the connections among the components.

Examples:

* who affects whom
* what depends on what
* in what order things are connected
* at which point the connection breaks or changes

At this stage, a simple list changes into a network of relations.

Here, relation does not mean merely recording the existence of a relation between individual elements. It also involves examining what position each connection occupies within the whole, what direction and conditions it has, and how it continues into or breaks from other connections.

At this stage, the existence and direction of connections are identified first. State changes and operating flows that arise through those connections are examined separately in the later transition stage.

### 2.4 Setting the boundary

Determine up to where something is to be regarded as the same structure.

Examples:

- inclusion range
- exclusion range
- inside and outside
- starting condition
- ending condition

Without this stage, the unit of analysis becomes unstable, and different layers easily become mixed.

### 2.5 Checking transition or flow

Examine whether the structure is merely a static arrangement or whether it includes state change.

Examples:

- initial state
- intermediate state
- transition condition
- resulting state
- repetition or branching

At this stage, a simple connection begins to be distinguished from an operating structure.

### 2.6 Extracting recurring patterns

Extract common forms repeated across multiple cases.

Examples:

- recurring bottlenecks
- frequently appearing branches
- common failure points
- common structures among similar cases

From this stage onward, individual cases are grouped at the level of pattern.

### 2.7 Checking exceptions and counterexamples

Check whether all cases have the same structure.

Examples:

- when it is not the same structure
- what conditions create exceptions
- what must change for it to become a different structure

This stage is necessary in order to reduce overgeneralization.

### 2.8 Summarizing and fixing the structure

Finally organize the result of the analysis.

Examples:

- core elements
- main relations
- boundaries
- transitions
- recurring patterns
- exception conditions

The form of organization may take various shapes, such as sentences, lists, tables, diagrams, or classification charts.

---

### 2.9 What “procedure” means in this sequence

The stages above do not mean a fixed checklist or algorithm that automatically performs structural analysis.

Structural analysis involves both a **formal procedure** and a **cognitive procedure**.

#### Formal procedure

A formal procedure provides a basic sequence for what to examine first and what to examine later in the analytical process.

Examples:

* define the object
* separate the components
* identify relations and connections
* set the boundary
* check transitions and flows

Such a sequence can be used as a framework for organizing the path of analysis.

#### Cognitive procedure

A cognitive procedure is the process of identifying, within the overall structure of the target, what should actually be regarded as an element, relation, boundary, transition, or exception at each stage.

For example, the existence of a formal stage called “identifying the relations” does not mean that structural analysis is performed merely by listing every relation. It is also necessary to recognize what position each connection occupies within the whole, what direction and conditions it has, and how it continues into or breaks from other connections.

Therefore, the analytical sequence in this document does **not mean that the actual structure is identified by the formal procedure alone**.

> The formal procedure provides the progression framework of structural analysis,
> while the cognitive procedure identifies the actual structure within that framework.

If the cognitive process is removed and only the fixed sequence remains, following the same stages may still produce a result different from structural analysis in its original sense.

---

## 3. One-line Compression

The general sequence of structural analysis may be summarized as follows.

> defining the object → separating the components → identifying the relations → setting the boundary → checking the transition → extracting patterns → checking exceptions → summarizing the structure

## 4. What Ordinary Users Commonly Call “Structure”

When ordinary users say “structure,” in many cases they are actually pointing to one of the following.

### 4.1 Form

Examples:

- table
- table of contents
- schematic
- diagram
- document arrangement

This may be a **form of expression of structure**, but does not by itself become structure itself.

### 4.2 Fixed procedure

Examples:

* step-by-step order
* checklist
* processing flow
* manual-style progression

Such procedures may serve as the formal framework or an expression of structural analysis.

However, following a fixed sequence alone cannot substitute for the cognitive process of identifying what should count as an element, relation, boundary, or transition at each stage.

Therefore, a fixed procedure may serve as the formal framework of structural analysis, but it does not by itself identify the actual structure, nor can it substitute for the cognitive process required for structural analysis.

### 4.3 Individual relation statements

Examples:

* A affects B
* C comes after B
* X and Y are connected

Such statements may represent a relation or connection between individual elements.

However, examining relations in structural analysis does not stop at listing relations individually. It also involves examining what position and direction each relation has within the whole, under what conditions it continues or breaks, and how it connects with other relations and transitions.

Therefore, an individual relation statement may express part of a structure, but is not immediately identical to structural analysis as a whole.

---

## 5. Why This Misunderstanding Arises

This misunderstanding arises because, in general, the following tend to look like structure.

* because they have a visible form
* because if there are steps, it feels like structure
* because if relations are explained, it feels sufficient

However, form, fixed procedure, and relation descriptions are all related to structure, but are usually closer to **expressions of structure or partial elements of it**.

That is, analyzing what appears to be structure and actually analyzing structure are not the same.

---

## 6. Minimal Entry Conditions of Structural Analysis

For structural analysis not to remain merely at the level of formal arrangement or listing procedures, at least the following elements are required.

### 6.1 See connections rather than objects

Rather than prioritizing the individual items themselves, prioritize the connections among them.

Here, connection does not mean merely the existence of relatedness. It also includes position, direction, dependency, conditions, possibility of disconnection, and continuation into other connections.

### 6.2 See position rather than value

Rather than value judgments such as good/bad or many/few, examine what position and role it has within the whole.

### 6.3 See transition conditions rather than results

Rather than the simple result, examine under what condition the state changes.

### 6.4 See actual operation rather than declared intention

Rather than the purpose or declaration stated on the surface, examine how it actually operates.

If these four elements are weak, the word “structure” easily remains an ornament or an abstract expression.

## 7. What This Document Covers and Does Not Cover

### 7.1 What this document covers

This document covers the following.

- the basic sequence of general structural analysis
- forms that are easily mistaken for structure
- the minimal conditions needed at the entry level of structural analysis

### 7.2 What this document does not cover

This document does not cover the following.

- the proprietary formalization method of a specific observational system
- higher-order coordinate design
- the definition and fixing method of lower observational units
- special tagging of transition points and cross structures
- the design of responsibility, judgment, and attribution structures within a specific methodology
- separate reinforcement layers, including cognitive-formative effects after formalization

That is, this document deals with **a shareable general entry**, and does not deal with **the proprietary structural package itself that is formalized in a specific way**.

## 8. Summary

The core of this document is as follows.

1. General structural analysis may proceed from fixing the object to separating the elements, identifying the relations, setting the boundary, checking the transition, extracting patterns, checking exceptions, and summarizing the structure.
2. What ordinary users call “structure” often remains at the level of form, fixed procedure, or relation descriptions.
3. Form, fixed procedure, and relation descriptions are related to structure, but are usually closer to expressions of structure or partial elements of it than to structure itself.
4. At the entry of structural analysis, it is necessary to examine relations, position, transition conditions, and actual operation together.
5. This document deals with the entry of general structural analysis, and does not deal with the proprietary formalization structure of any specific methodology.
